MWhat I hate about the SAT

For anyone who hasn’t taken the SAT, this post is simply a rant about how unfair the SAT has been. To be clear, the SAT is still a good test to polish your university applications. However, there are serious downsides in the way the College Board – the organization that administers the test – has been managing it.

The SAT is designed as a critical thinking exam, covering both math and verbal sections. If we focus solely on the topics it tests, I have to admit that the SAT does a great job assessing students’ reasoning skills. The verbal module, for example, is largely argument-based. It also features questions about writing conventions and vocabulary, which is why it’s called “verbal” rather than just “reading.” The verbel section tests students’ ability to synthesize information and logically reason through the context – it’s like debate training without requiring deep expertise in any particular field. This allows students to have their logical thinking assessed fairly.

The math module, on the other hand, is mostly precalculus. It rigorously tests a student’s core understanding of basic concepts. This is one of the strengths of the SAT because it focuses on fundamentals, meaning students don’t need to spend months learning obscure content. Instead, it challenges them to think creatively and apply foundational knowledge to solve complex problems.

Another strength of the SAT is its grading system. Since all questions are multiple-choice with four options, luck inevitably plays a role. You could, theoretically, guess your way through and score high. To minimize this, the SAT uses a curve grading system that penalizes mistakes on easy questions more heavily than mistakes on hard ones. In theory, if a student correctly guesses a hard question but misses several easy ones, it exposes gaps in their true understanding.

However, the rotten core of the SAT lies in the verbal section. College Board has a notorious habit of recycling old questions. Despite being a massive organization, they don’t seem to hire enough people to consistently create fresh question sets for each test date. On average, you might find 20–40% of the hard questions recycled from older exams. Although it’s technically illegal to publicly release official test materials, leaks inevitably happen.

A clear example of this issue happened recently in Vietnam. On March 8, students taking the SAT faced a system error that automatically submitted their tests before the time was up. In response to this global technical failure, the College Board offered students a refund, the choice to cancel or keep their scores, and the opportunity to retake the test. At first, this seemed fair, but it actually exposed the major flaw of recycling questions from past tests. This gave students a chance to retest with prior knowledge to what questions that would likely appear. Students who get to retake the exam can easily look up leaked questions online and memorize the answers, rather than relying on real critical thinking skills. For me, this situation completely devalues the exam and undermines its original purpose of assessing students’ reasoning abilities. After the make-up exam, some even reported that they had all of their questons identical to the old tests. It was honestly obnoxious to see so many people take unfair advantage of what should have been an justified opportunity to assess themselves academically.

Before the make-up exam, social media was deeply divided. Some students, aware they didn’t have the same advantage, urged others to report the leaks to the College Board in hopes that a brand-new question set would be created. Others argued it wasn’t their responsibility to fight for a fairer exam. Personally, I don’t think there was a clear right or wrong in this situation – it’s hard to say how many people would stick to their principles if they had a real shot at retaking the test with a leaked advantage. However, what I do despise are those who shamelessly boasted online about benefiting from the leaks while criticizing others for demanding fairness. Flaunting their unfair advantage turned them into nothing more than imbeciles. Honestly, given their arrogance and mediocrity, I doubt half of them could score well even with leaked questions handed to them.

Still, I expected better – especially from a test as globally significant as the SAT. A test that claims to assess critical thinking should uphold higher standards of integrity. Based on the College Board’s reputation alone, they should have invested more resources into creating fresh question sets – not just for the March 8 makeup exam, but for every test date. It’s not hard to see that recycling old materials invites leaks, score inflation, and ultimately cheapens the value of the entire exam.

At the end of the day, the SAT has its merits, but moments like these reveal just how fragile its credibility can be.
